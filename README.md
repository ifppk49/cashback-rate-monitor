# cashback monitor: How to Compare Rates Before You Buy and Keep Your Cashback From Going Missing

Type "cashback monitor" into a search box and you get two completely different things mashed together. Half the results are comparison tools that watch rates across dozens of shopping portals. The other half are extensions and apps that claim to watch those same rates for you while you shop. Some of them share a name, which makes the whole category more confusing than it needs to be.

There's also a third job almost nobody advertises properly: monitoring your own cashback after you've bought something. That's where most of the actual money leaks out. You got the 8% rate, great. Now it sits pending for six weeks, the retailer reports the order as returned, and you find out three months later that the $12 you were counting on never existed.

This article covers all three jobs, what each one is actually good for, and where ShopBack does and doesn't fit into a monitoring routine.

## What a cashback monitor is supposed to do

Ignore the branding for a second and look at the functions. A monitor does four things, and most tools only really do two of them well.

| Job | What it looks like in practice | Where it usually breaks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compare rates across portals | One store page showing what Rakuten, TopCashBack, BeFrugal, airline portals and card portals each offer today | Only as current as the site's last crawl, and "rate" ignores exclusions |
| Show rate history | A chart of how a store's best offer moved over the past year or so, so you can judge whether today is a good day to buy | History tells you about the past, not whether the offer will hold through your order |
| Alert you when a rate hits a target | Email or push notification when your store reaches a threshold you set | Requires an account, a target, and patience |
| Track your own payout | Pending → confirmed → withdrawable, with a date attached to each stage | Comparison tools generally don't do this at all |

Bankrate's guide to Cashback Monitor describes a site that tracks earning rates across dozens of portals, with updates roughly once a day, plus a "best rate history" view covering the previous 15 months and custom alerts on up to eight portals and twenty retailers. Savewise, which The Points Guy and CNBC Select have both written about, does a similar comparison job across 29 portals and layers card-linked offers on top, with offer history going back up to 18 months.

That's the rate-watching half of the category solved. The payout half is usually left to whatever cashback platform you actually clicked through, which is why the two halves deserve separate attention.

### The naming mess, briefly

There's CashbackMonitor.com, the aggregator. There's also a browser extension literally called Cashback Monitor, which has a few hundred users and asks for access to your tabs. And "cashback monitor" is used generically for anything that watches rates, including extension badges that pop up when you land on a retail site.

None of that is dangerous on its own, but it's worth reading permissions and install counts before handing a browser extension your browsing session. An extension with a few hundred users is a support risk, not a scam, and in the cashback world the difference matters.

## The rate on the page is not the rate in your account

Here's the part that comparison tools can't fix. A posted percentage is a ceiling, not a promise, and the gap between the two is where people get annoyed.

Cashback at any legitimate platform is a share of the affiliate commission the merchant pays for sending the sale. ShopBack's own explainer puts it plainly: you pay the merchant's normal checkout price, the commission comes out of the retailer's existing marketing budget, and the cashback stays pending until the merchant confirms the order is final.

Three things shrink that number:

- **Excluded items.** Gift cards, vouchers and store credit typically earn nothing. Neither do shipping, taxes and service charges. In many programs, using a promo code that isn't listed on the platform invalidates the order entirely.
- **Return windows.** Cashback usually confirms after the retailer's return window closes. Retail orders commonly clear in 30 to 90 days; travel bookings clear after the trip.
- **Small orders on flat-rate merchants.** Some stores don't pay a percentage at all. Amazon's terms on ShopBack, as last published, have been $1 for a first-ever tracked purchase and $0.50 for additional orders, capped at five cashback rewards per month. A 3% headline rate at a different merchant on a $200 order beats that comfortably.

ShopBack's own worked example is the right mental model. An $80 checkout with a verified 5% offer produces $4 in later, conditional value. The merchant still charges $80 today. If the order is excluded or returned, your cost stays $80. Budget the full amount, treat cashback as a conditional return that arrives later.

Which leads to a simple rule when you're staring at a monitor deciding whether to wait: the discount comes off the price now, in full, and cashback comes later as a percentage. Waiting for a higher rate makes sense for a large purchase you're not in a hurry to complete. It stops making sense when you're risking a sale price, stock, or a delivery window to chase an extra percentage point on a $40 order.

## A 60-second routine that beats any dashboard

Monitoring cashback is mostly about not breaking attribution. The tools are cheap; the discipline is what pays.

1. Open the store page on ShopBack. Read the rate and the exclusions, not just the percentage.
2. If you use an aggregator, convert everything to cash before comparing. Points and miles need a cents-per-point value or you're comparing currencies.
3. Build the cart and get to the checkout page. Note the total. Stop there.
4. Return to ShopBack, activate the offer, and complete the purchase in that same session, on that same device and browser. Don't search for a better price mid-flow, don't open a banner ad, don't wander into another coupon site.
5. Pay with your rewards card, and use only codes the merchant or ShopBack published.
6. Save the order confirmation. Check for tracked cashback within 48 hours and file a claim if it's missing.

The failure modes are boringly consistent: ad blockers and privacy extensions interfering with tracking cookies, coupon extensions injecting their own referral at the payment step, switching from app to desktop halfway through, paying with a gift card, and shopping in incognito. On that last one, ShopBack specifically documents that incognito mode breaks tracking at Marriott, so it isn't a universal rule, but it's a documented exception worth knowing about.

## Questions people ask before trusting a monitor

**Is there a catch if it's free?** No hidden fee, but there is a business model. Merchants pay for referred sales, the platform keeps part of that commission and passes the rest to you. Nothing is added to your checkout price. The catch isn't cost, it's dependency: if tracking breaks, nobody gets paid.

**Do I need the extension?** No. You can earn by starting at the website or app every time. But the complaint that shows up most often in app store reviews is cashback that takes longer than expected to verify, and the second most common is forgetting to route a purchase at all. The extension addresses the second problem directly.

**Does a cashback monitor replace my credit card strategy?** No, and it shouldn't. Cashback, merchant promo codes and card rewards come out of three different budgets, so they stack. The order matters: activate cashback first, apply merchant or platform codes second, pay with the rewards card last.

**Why does my rate differ from what the monitor showed?** Because rates change by merchant category and by date, and app rates can differ from web rates for the same store. The live figure on the merchant's page inside the platform you're actually using is the one that counts.
